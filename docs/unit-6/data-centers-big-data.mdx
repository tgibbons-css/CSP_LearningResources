## Part 1: How Big Data drives physical infrastructure

### From a spreadsheet to a server cluster

A small table can fit on a laptop. A service receiving many simultaneous requests, storing many copies of media, or training a large model may need much more compute, storage, and network capacity. Distributed systems spread work across machines, but those machines still need racks, cables, electricity, maintenance, and cooling. [Cloudflare’s cloud overview](https://www.cloudflare.com/learning/cloud/what-is-the-cloud/) connects remote services to the physical servers that deliver them.

Scale creates pressure; it does not make one enormous building the only possible design. Workload reduction, efficient software, location choices, and sharing or distributing resources also matter.

### Track 1: The 5 V’s of Big Data

Use **Volume** (amount), **Velocity** (arrival and processing rate), **Variety** (formats), **Veracity** (trustworthiness), and **Value** (useful outcomes) to describe a workload. Which V increases resource needs? Which might help us decide whether the work is worth doing?

- [IBM: What is Big Data?](https://www.ibm.com/topics/big-data)
- [Oracle: What Is Big Data?](https://www.oracle.com/big-data/what-is-big-data/)
- [Mobile CSP: 7.2 Big Data](https://runestone.academy/ns/books/published/mobilecsp/Unit7-Using-Analyzing-Data/Big-Data.html)
- [YouTube: Simplilearn - What is Big Data? 5 V's Explained](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bAyrObl7TYE)

### Track 2: Structured, semi-structured, and unstructured data

Compare a table with consistent columns, a JSON record with keys and nested values, and an image or audio file without that tabular schema. “Unstructured” does not mean meaningless. JSON has structure even when records vary. AI workloads can combine structured records, JSON, text, and media; **format alone does not determine size**.

- [AWS: Structured Data vs. Unstructured Data](https://aws.amazon.com/compare/the-difference-between-structured-data-and-unstructured-data/)
- [MDN: Working with JSON](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Learn_web_development/Core/Scripting/JSON)
- [YouTube: IBM Technology - Structured vs Unstructured Data Explained](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eK3I6oI_4Is)

### Track 3: Distributed data storage & cloud infrastructure

Parallel computing performs work concurrently. Distributed computing coordinates machines connected by a network. Cluster storage spreads data across machines and may replicate it for resilience. Adding machines can increase capacity, but communication, coordination, and network bottlenecks can limit the benefit. Use the sources below to connect these ideas to physical infrastructure.

- [Cloudflare: What Is the Cloud?](https://www.cloudflare.com/learning/cloud/what-is-the-cloud/)
- [AWS: What Is Cloud Computing?](https://aws.amazon.com/what-is-cloud-computing/)
- [AWS: What Is Distributed Computing?](https://aws.amazon.com/what-is/distributed-computing/)
- [Microsoft MakeCode CSP: Parallel and Distributed Computing](https://microsoft.github.io/makecode-csp/unit-2/day-6/parallel-distributed-computing/)
- [YouTube: IBM Technology - Distributed Computing Explained](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qJb33Lp8U5U)

## Part 2: Unmasking the “invisible cloud” — the Hermantown jigsaw

The cloud can feel weightless when we tap an app. Its physical infrastructure occupies land and uses resources. Now examine Google’s proposed hyperscale data center in Hermantown, in St. Louis County, Minnesota. Your task is to reason from evidence, not to assume that the project is either inherently beneficial or inherently harmful.

:::info A dated case study, not a live project tracker
The pod fact cards use March–June 2026 sources, checked October 14, 2026. MPR’s June 25 reporting describes an approximately **$2 billion**, roughly **280-acre** proposal near **Midway Road and Morris Thomas Road**. The City’s March 31 scoping notice describes a **278-acre study area**. Consult the [City’s project and environmental-review page](https://hermantownmn.com/project/) for later documents and decisions; the earlier readings do not establish today’s approval status.
:::

### How the jigsaw works

1. Form four expert pods: energy, water, economic/compute value, and community/land use.
2. Each pod uses its questions and linked sources to prepare **one supported claim, one uncertainty, and one proposed condition**. Distinguish reported statements from measured outcomes.
3. Re-form into home groups with one representative from each pod. Share evidence before using the estimator.
4. Hold compute load constant while changing cooling architecture, then hold cooling constant while changing compute load. What changes? What does the model leave out?
5. Reach a consensus on **three measurable conditions** for the City and utility regulators. Include evidence and monitoring responsibilities, then copy the group reflection for your submission destination.

:::warning Do not confuse supply capacity with demand
[Minnesota Power’s announcement](https://hermantownmn.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/PressRelease03032026.pdf) describes **300 MW wind + 400 MW battery capacity**, totaling 700 MW of new resources. Batteries store energy rather than generating it. The announcement is not a confirmed 700 MW site load, a promise of 700 MW every hour, or a statement of battery storage duration. MW measures power; MWh measures energy over time.
:::

### Estimator math: power, energy, and water

The estimator assumes a constant IT load for 24 hours: **IT kWh/day = IT MW * 1,000 * 24**. **PUE = facility energy / IT energy**, so facility MW = IT MW * PUE. The scenarios use PUE **1.12** (standard evaporative), **1.15** (hot-day evaporative), and **1.25** (air/closed-loop). Overhead includes cooling and other facility systems.

**WUE = water liters / IT kWh**. The evaporative scenarios use **0.85** and **1.8 L/kWh**; gallons/day = round(IT kWh/day * WUE / 3.78541). The dry air/closed-loop scenario has **zero evaporative server-cooling water**, with domestic/staff and humidification use modeled separately as round(8,000 + IT MW * 25) gal/day. At 100 MW, these scenarios give about **539,000**, **1.14 million**, and **10,500 gal/day**, respectively. The homes comparison uses total facility kW / **1.2 kW per household**, rounded to whole homes.

These are illustrative classroom coefficients, not ISO-prescribed performance values, verified national averages, or measured Hermantown design values. [ISO/IEC's PUE standard](https://www.iso.org/standard/30134-2) and [WUE standard](https://www.iso.org/standard/77692.html) define the metrics; [LBNL's 2024 report](https://eta-publications.lbl.gov/sites/default/files/2024-12/us_data_center_energy_usage_report_lbnl-2001637_0.pdf) provides national modeling context. A daily scenario is not an annual measured KPI. Open **How is this calculated? (WUE & PUE Math)** below to trace the conversions. The separate indirect-water note applies an illustrative 1.5-2.0 gal/kWh thermal-generation sensitivity to **facility** electricity, not IT electricity; actual off-site consumption depends on the electricity supply and plant cooling systems.
